1 · The question

A sprint, and five ways it can go wrong

At the start of a sprint a Scrum team commits to a set of issues. When the planned end comes, some issues may still be open, and someone may close the sprint in Jira days late. Press play to watch one made-up sprint, then see which of the paper's five outcomes it triggers.

The study asks whether these outcomes can be seen coming, at planning time or a quarter or half way through the sprint, from what Jira already recorded at that moment.

2 · The trap

A Jira export remembers how the sprint ended, not how it began

A public Jira dataset is a snapshot taken years later. The sprint field and the story points it shows are the final values. Train on them and the model quietly learns from things that did not exist at planning. The fix is to replay each issue's change log back to the sprint's first day. The measurability check, the paper's quiet bug fix

Spillover means "an issue was not finished by the planned end", and "finished" comes from the issue's resolution date. In three projects most sprint issues are closed with no resolution date. A rule that reads only that date counts them as never finished, so those sprints look like disasters that never happened.

The confirmation set

The hypotheses were written after a development run on eight projects. Seven of those are in the cohort. The other 19 projects had never been looked at, so every hypothesis test is repeated on them alone. A result that holds on both is much harder to explain away as tuning to the data.

Protocol. Gradient boosting, trained on the first 70% of each project's sprints by start date and tested on the last 30% (1,118 test sprints). Every comparison is a paired AUC difference with a 95% interval from resampling whole projects. A hypothesis holds only if that interval is above zero. In-sprint models are scored at 25% and 50% of the planned duration.

5 · The theory

Four small results that predict what the data should show

The formal part defines an admissible feature (it uses only the change log up to the prediction time) and a measurable outcome (every committed issue either has a resolution date or is still open). Four consequences follow, each checked against the data.

Result 1 (Proposition 1): empty sprints inflate AUC by an exact amount

A sprint that commits nothing cannot spill, so it is a negative by construction and any model ranks it last. Keep those sprints in the test set and the AUC goes up for free.

Result 2 (Proposition 2): a fitted threshold does not travel

Youden's threshold quietly assumes that a miss costs (1 − π)/π false alarms, where π is the share of bad sprints in training. Move to a team with fewer bad sprints and the same threshold misses most of them. On the outside projects, the share of late sprints the threshold catches tracks each project's prevalence (rank correlation 0.93): from 86% down to none at all.

Result 3: a binomial model of sprint completion

Suppose each committed issue finishes by the planned end with probability q, a property of the team. Then "anything left over" is almost certain once a sprint has a few issues, so scope should explain plain spillover. But "a lot left over" depends on q, so team history should explain serious spillover. Play with it.

P(anything left over) = 1 − qⁿ

At the cohort's median, q ≈ 0.33 and a three-issue sprint already spills with probability 0.963. The data agree: history beats scope by 0.071 AUC for serious spillover and by −0.003 for plain spillover.

Result 4 (Proposition 3): issues do not finish independently

A blocked dependency or an underestimated epic holds back several issues at once. The beta-binomial model adds one number, the correlation r between issues of a sprint, and predicts that the binomial variance is too small by a factor that grows with sprint size. Fitted r = 0.18. The gap is 1.7× for sprints of one to three issues and 5.8× for 16 or more.

What a warning is worth

AUC says nothing about whether acting on a warning pays. Net benefit does: it counts the failures a warning catches minus the false alarms, priced by how sure you must be before acting (the threshold p*). Never warning scores zero.

7 · So what

What a team can take from this

  1. During a sprint, count. How much is done, how much is left, and how fast it is going carry nearly all of the signal, and their warnings are calibrated. Burndown-shape indicators add nothing on top.
  2. Before a sprint, history tells teams apart, not a team's sprints. Across many teams, each team's recent completion rate is the best guide to how much will be left over, and the binomial model gets most of the way with one number per team. Within one team it adds little to the size of the sprint: the serious-spillover gain falls from 0.088 to 0.015 AUC when only pairs of sprints from the same project are compared.
  3. Be careful importing a model or a threshold from another organisation. We saw no sign that either carried over, and a threshold changes what a miss is worth when the share of bad sprints changes.
  4. For outcomes as common as spillover, warning on every sprint is almost as good as any model unless warnings are costly.
  5. Late closure is mostly noise. It records when someone pressed "Complete sprint", and nothing tried here predicts it well.

Where a reviewer will push

  • The labels come from Jira fields, not from asking teams whether a sprint went badly. No practitioner has yet judged the flagged sprints.
  • Only 26 project clusters, so project-level intervals are wide. Only 5 of 22 tests survive Holm.
  • Some analysis choices were made after the full run (the measurability check, the confirmation set, the Holm step). All are logged with dates, and the confirmation set is the main defence, though its 19 projects are part of the full cohort, so it is not an independent replication.
  • Every AUC in this literature is pooled over projects, which also rewards telling teams apart. The paper now scores the main comparisons within projects too, and most of the planning-time history gain disappears there.
  • 21% of issues placed in a sprint have no sprint history, so the replay has to assume their exported sprint. The main results hold on the 15 projects with at least 85% coverage.
  • All data are open source, and a handful of organisations contribute most TAWOS projects.
8 · Words used

Glossary

ROC-AUC
The chance that the model gives a random bad sprint a higher risk than a random good one. 0.5 is guessing, 1 is perfect.
Carry ratio
The share of committed issues still unfinished at the planned end.
Spillover / serious spillover
Carry ratio above zero / at least 30% (a median-based version is reported too).
Late closure / seriously late
Sprint closed in Jira more than one / three days after its planned end.
Scope churn
Issues added after the start make up 30% or more of the committed count.
Leave-one-project-out (LOPO)
Train on all projects but one, test on that one. Repeated for every project.
Holm adjustment
A correction for running 22 tests: the more tests, the stronger each result must be.
Calibration slope
1 means a predicted 70% really happens about 70% of the time. Below 1 means the probabilities are too extreme.
Net benefit
Caught failures minus false alarms weighted by the decision threshold, per sprint. Zero is never warning.
Every number on this page comes from the result files in results/tawos_all_clean/. The sprint and replay animations are made-up examples to explain the method.
